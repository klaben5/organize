How much does employee turnover really cost? A formula for sales teams

A sales exit costs far more than the price of a job ad. Add up recruiting, training, the months a new hire spends ramping, and the revenue lost while the seat is empty, and one exit often costs more than the person's annual base pay.

Most companies can tell you their revenue to the dollar, but almost none can tell you what turnover cost them last year. That's because the costs are spread across budgets: recruiting sits in HR, training sits with operations, and lost deals just show up as a missed forecast. Here's how to pull them into one number.

What goes into the cost of a sales exit?

Every exit has four costs:

  1. Recruiting. Job ads, recruiter fees, and the hours managers spend screening and interviewing.
  2. Training. Onboarding time, trainer or manager time, and materials.
  3. Ramp gap. The revenue a new hire doesn't produce while they get up to speed, compared with a fully ramped rep.
  4. Vacancy loss. The revenue lost while the seat is empty, minus whatever teammates cover.

Recruiting and training are the costs people usually count. For sales teams, the ramp gap and vacancy loss are usually the big ones.

What is the formula?

Cost per exit = recruiting + training + (monthly revenue per rep × months vacant) + (monthly revenue per rep × months to ramp × 50%)

The 50% is a simple assumption: a new hire produces about half of a ramped rep's output, on average, across their ramp period. Swap in your own ramp curve if you have one.

Then:

Annual cost of turnover = cost per exit × exits per year

What does that look like with real numbers?

Here's a worked example for a hypothetical retail sales team. These aren't benchmarks, just round numbers to show the math.

CostAssumptionAmount
RecruitingAds, screening, interview time$3,000
TrainingTwo weeks of onboarding$4,000
Vacancy loss$20,000/month in sales × 1 month$20,000
Ramp gap$20,000/month × 4 months × 50%$40,000

That's $7,000 in direct costs and $60,000 in lost sales. Revenue isn't profit, so turn the lost sales into margin: at a 45% gross margin, $60,000 of lost sales is $27,000 of lost margin. Add the $7,000 in recruiting and training and this team's cost per exit is about $34,000.

With 40 exits a year, that's $1.36 million. Cutting exits by a quarter is worth $340,000 a year, before counting the effect on the team that stays.

Why do most leaders undercount it?

  • They count recruiting and training, which are visible, and skip ramp and vacancy, which aren't on any invoice.
  • They treat every exit the same. A rep who leaves at day 60 costs you recruiting and training with almost no sales in return.
  • They miss the knock-on costs: the manager's time, and the teammates who cover the gap and start to burn out.

How do I calculate my own number?

  1. Pull your exits from the last 12 months and your average headcount.
  2. Estimate monthly revenue for a fully ramped rep, your typical months to fill a seat, and months to full ramp.
  3. Plug them into the formula above, then multiply by your gross margin.

Frequently asked questions

Is turnover cost just the cost of hiring a replacement?

No. Recruiting is usually the smallest piece. For sales roles, ramp time and revenue lost during the vacancy are typically much larger.

Should I count voluntary and involuntary exits?

Count both, but track them separately. Both cost money, but they have different causes and different fixes.

What if I don't know my exact numbers?

Start with rough estimates. A reasonable number that leadership agrees on is far more useful than no number at all.
